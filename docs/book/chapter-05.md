# Chapter 5
## Do the Numbers Work?

Buying a property for £100,000 and selling it for £130,000 does not mean you made £30,000.

You have to pay for buying it, owning it, doing the work and selling it.

What is left belongs to you. Everything before that belongs to the deal.

This is where a promising property earns its place on your shortlist—or comes off it.

You have already looked at the area, the rent or sale evidence, and the things that could make the property difficult. Now put a price against the plan.

**If the deal only works when you leave costs out, it does not work.**

### Start with the whole cost

People often lead with the purchase price and the refurbishment.

Those are two important numbers. They are not the whole project.

Work through the costs in stages.

| Stage | What to include |
|---|---|
| Buying | Price, applicable purchase tax, legal work, searches, surveys, valuations, broker and sourcing fees |
| Funding | Interest, arrangement fees, lender legal costs, exit charges and any other product costs |
| Improving | Labour, materials, relevant VAT, professional fees, approvals, waste removal and connections |
| Holding | Insurance, utilities, applicable council tax or business rates, security and other running costs |
| Letting or selling | Agent fees, legal work, setup costs, marketing and any other exit costs |
| Contingency | A separate allowance for uncertainty in the work and programme |

Some items will not apply to your purchase. Others will need more detail.

The exercise is to find your actual costs, rather than copy a list and assume the total is complete.

Check whether quotes include VAT. Check whether a finance fee is paid upfront, deducted from the advance or added to the loan. Count each cost once, and put it in the right place in the cash plan.

Two quotes for “the same price” can leave you with different amounts to pay.

### Give every important number a source

The purchase price might be known. The refurbishment may still be a rough allowance. The rent might come from three comparable properties and two agent conversations.

Those figures deserve different levels of confidence.

Alongside each important number, write where it came from:

- An agreed figure or written quote.
- A calculated estimate based on identified evidence.
- An allowance that still needs checking.

A spreadsheet makes all three look equally tidy. They are not equally dependable.

Do not spend hours adjusting the colour of a report while leaving the biggest cost as “probably about twenty grand”.

If the work is the main uncertainty, improving the scope and quotes will help more than another evening browsing listings.

### A flip: where did the margin go?

Here is an illustrative deal. These are made-up figures to show the calculation, not a quote for a particular property.

You believe the finished house could sell for £150,000. Your comparable evidence supports investigating that figure.

The purchase price is £100,000.

At first glance, that looks like a £50,000 gap.

Now put the costs in.

| Item | Amount |
|---|---:|
| Purchase | £100,000 |
| Buying costs and applicable purchase tax | £8,000 |
| Refurbishment | £20,000 |
| Finance costs | £5,000 |
| Holding costs | £2,000 |
| Selling costs | £3,000 |
| Separate contingency allowance | £4,000 |
| Total budget | £142,000 |
| Expected sale proceeds before the above costs | £150,000 |
| Budgeted profit before tax | £8,000 |

That is a very different conversation from “buy at a hundred, sell at a hundred and fifty”.

The £8,000 allows for the contingency being spent. If you do not spend it, the result improves. But do not release it in your mind before the uncertainty has passed.

Now imagine the sale completes at £145,000 and the project incurs another £2,000 of finance and holding costs because it takes longer.

The budget becomes £144,000 against a £145,000 sale.

You are left with £1,000 before tax.

A £5,000 change in the sale price and £2,000 in extra costs have used up almost all the expected profit.

That does not mean every flip is bad. It means you need enough margin for the work, time and uncertainty you are taking on.

### Work backwards to the price you can pay

Suppose you want £20,000 of profit before tax from that illustrative project.

The supported sale estimate is still £150,000. The other budgeted costs, including contingency, total £42,000.

The first-pass calculation is:

**£150,000 sale − £42,000 other costs − £20,000 required profit = £88,000 purchase price.**

That gives you a starting offer ceiling.

Some costs change with the purchase price or loan amount, so recalculate the full appraisal at £88,000. Do not assume the first subtraction is the final answer.

But it has already told you something useful: a £100,000 asking price does not fit the return you want on those assumptions.

You can investigate whether the costs or exit estimate are wrong. You can negotiate. You can leave it alone.

What you cannot sensibly do is decide that you will somehow get the missing profit back later.

**The asking price is the seller's starting point. Your appraisal gives you yours.**

### Clare Street: separate the gap from the profit

Remember Clare Street.

We bought for £95,000 cash and completed the resale at £120,000 about three months later.

The difference was £25,000. After legal and other deal costs, the investor made roughly £20,000 before tax.

There was no major refurbishment. That was a big part of why the gap survived as profit.

Compare that with a property offering the same £25,000 gap but needing a £20,000 refurbishment before you even pay the buying and selling costs.

The gap is the same. The deal is completely different.

This is why “below market value” needs context. Below what supported value, in what condition, and after spending how much?

### A rental: yield is the beginning

I like cash flow. So when I look at a rental, I want to understand what it can leave after its costs.

Start with gross yield:

**Annual rent ÷ purchase price × 100.**

A £100,000 house renting for £800 a month produces £9,600 a year.

That is a 9.6% gross yield on the purchase price.

It is not a 9.6% return landing in your bank account. It has not deducted running costs or finance. It also ignores the extra money you spent buying and improving the property.

If the total project cost is £125,000, the same £9,600 rent represents 7.68% of that cost before running costs and finance.

Both calculations can be useful. Label them properly so you know what you are comparing.

### What does it leave each month?

Take that £800 monthly rent and build an illustrative operating budget.

| Monthly item | Amount |
|---|---:|
| Rent when occupied and paid | £800 |
| Allowance for empty periods and unpaid rent | −£40 |
| Management allowance | −£80 |
| Repairs and future replacement reserve | −£100 |
| Insurance and other running costs | −£40 |
| Cash available before mortgage payments | £540 |
| Illustrative interest-only mortgage payment | −£350 |
| Budgeted cash flow before tax | £190 |

Those allowances are examples. Get the management quote, insurance terms and finance costs for your property. Add anything else you will pay.

If you use a repayment mortgage, put the full payment into the cash-flow calculation. The capital repayment reduces debt, but the cash still leaves your account.

And remember what an allowance means. You are not paying £100 to a boiler company every month. You are budgeting for costs that arrive unevenly.

If you withdraw every penny because nothing broke this month, do not be surprised when a later repair takes the money back.

### Twenty rooms does not mean twenty rents

Our Swansea HMOs make this point painfully well.

At the time of writing, eight of twenty rooms across four houses are available.

A calculation that assumes twenty paying occupiers would give us a comforting answer to the wrong question.

Model the rent you can reasonably expect to collect. Include the bills and costs you pay even while rooms are empty.

Then look at the property as a whole. HMO bills, cleaning, management, maintenance and empty rooms can produce a very different result from multiplying one room's rent by the number of bedrooms.

For serviced accommodation, do the same with nightly rates, occupancy, cleaning, booking fees, utilities and other operating costs. Use a sensible annual picture, with enough cash to cope with the quieter months.

**A full building is an operating outcome. It is not a starting assumption you get for free.**

### A valuation is not money released

At Trebanos, we bought two houses for £130,000 together. After the work, they were valued at £260,000 combined. Later valuations totalled £350,000.

Those figures matter. But the valuation does not tell you how much cash a refinance will put back in your account.

For an illustration, imagine a £260,000 valuation and a lender willing to advance 75% of that value.

That would be a £195,000 gross loan.

You would then need to account for the existing borrowing being repaid, any retained or deducted fees and the other refinance costs.

The money left after those deductions is what may become available to you.

Also compare it with all the cash you put into the project. Purchase, works, fees and holding costs all matter.

A higher valuation is useful. A finance product that does not release the money your plan needs can still leave your capital tied up.

Check the product, eligibility, valuation basis and net proceeds before relying on a refinance exit.

### Can you fund the journey?

A deal can show a profit at the end and still run out of cash halfway through.

You might need to pay a deposit, tax, fees and early works before a lender releases a later stage of funding. A contractor may need payment before a refinance completes.

Map out when the money leaves and when it becomes available.

Keep household money, committed project cash and contingency separate. If your plan relies on another investor, a sale or a loan that is not yet agreed, name that dependency.

“We should have the money by then” is not the same as having funding in place.

Find the point at which the project needs the most cash. Can you cover it, including a reasonable delay?

### Mozarts: when running late costs £50,000

Our 26-bedroom HMO project, Mozarts, has gone far enough over programme that we need a replacement development-finance product.

At the time of writing, we expect that change to cost us roughly £50,000 or more.

Around £30,000 is setup and other additional fees. Then there is another £20,000-plus in interest.

| Additional finance cost | Approximate amount |
|---|---:|
| Setup and other extra fees | £30,000 |
| Additional interest | £20,000-plus |
| Total expected additional cost | £50,000-plus |

That is money the project now has to absorb because it has taken longer than planned.

The building has not gained another bedroom because we paid those fees. The extra interest has not bought us a better kitchen. It is the cost of funding the longer journey.

On a bigger project, a delay can mean much more than another month's interest. If it takes you beyond the existing finance term, you may need a new product, another set of fees and more time before the intended exit.

So ask two questions when you test a delay: **what does the extra time cost, and what happens if the current finance runs out before we finish?**

Then establish when those costs must be paid. Fees deducted from a new advance can leave less money available for the remaining work. Interest added to a loan still has to be repaid at the exit.

This is why the programme, finance term and cash plan belong together. On larger projects, the amounts can become substantial very quickly.

### Make the deal answer three harder questions

Before relying on your base appraisal, run three alternatives:

1. **The result is lower.** What if the rent, sale price or refinance valuation disappoints?
2. **The work costs more.** What if you uncover repairs beyond the allowance?
3. **The project takes longer.** What extra finance, bills and lost income follow?

Then combine setbacks. A delay and a cost increase can arrive together.

Choose changes that make sense for the property. There is no magic percentage that makes every appraisal safe.

Look at what happens to profit, monthly cash flow and the cash you must leave in. Does the result still justify the commitment? Can you cope if the weaker outcome happens?

That is the purpose of the exercise: to make a decision while you can still change the price or walk away.

### Put Property Predator to work

Property Predator runs the appraisal and produces a report across the strategy you are considering.

### Your next move

Take the strongest property from your shortlist and complete one whole appraisal.

Record:

- The supported rent or sale estimate.
- The purchase, work, funding, holding and exit costs.
- The separate contingency.
- The cash needed to get through the project.
- The base result and the weaker alternatives.
- The maximum purchase price that meets your requirements.

Use **Example 08: Run the whole appraisal** as the companion topic reference at **martinhowardproperty.co.uk/book/companion/**. Keep the completed appraisal with your viewing notes and comparison checklist.

Do not force the result. If the price does not work, that is useful information. Finding out before you buy has cost you far less than finding out afterwards.

You now have a plan you can explain and numbers you can challenge.

There is still something that can stop the whole thing.

The spreadsheet may assume you can build it, use it, let it or finance it in a particular way. What if one of those assumptions is not allowed—or nobody will fund it?

**A profitable plan on paper is only useful if you can actually carry it out.**
