# Chapter 6
## What Could Stop This Working?

The numbers work.

You have a sensible purchase price, a costed refurbishment and evidence for the rent or sale value.

Lovely.

Now tell me which assumption could stop the whole thing.

Perhaps you need permission to create the extra unit. Perhaps the rooms on your drawing do not make a workable HMO. Perhaps the rear access belongs to somebody else. Perhaps the lender likes the building but will not fund the way you intend to use it.

These are not reasons to stop looking for opportunities. They are questions to answer while you still have choices.

**A property can be cheap for a reason. Your job is to find out whether you can resolve that reason at a price that works.**

### Start with the thing that makes the deal work

Write down the main source of value in your plan.

“I will refurbish it and let it as a normal house.”

“I will convert the upper floors into flats.”

“I will create an HMO.”

“I will extend the property and sell it.”

Now ask: what has to be true for that to happen?

For a straightforward refurbishment, the unanswered questions may mainly concern the condition, costs and finance.

For a conversion, you may need a suitable planning route, a workable design, legal rights, adequate services and funding that matches the project.

Find the dependency that matters most. Give it attention first.

If the whole profit depends on creating another dwelling, do not spend your first week choosing its bathroom before establishing whether the dwelling is feasible.

### Property myth: someone else has checked it

There are several versions of this.

“The agent said you could do it.”

“The seller already had drawings.”

“The lender is happy.”

“The solicitor will pick it up.”

Each person may have dealt with one part of the picture. None of those statements tells you that the complete investment plan has been checked.

Your solicitor needs to know which access or use you are relying on. Your broker needs the actual property and proposed strategy. The designer needs to understand the outcome you are trying to achieve and the budget it must fit.

Make sure the people helping you are answering the same question.

A report on the existing house is not automatically an assessment of your proposed conversion.

**Give your team the plan you intend to carry out.**

### “Potential” is not a planning decision

A large garden may have potential. So may an empty upper floor or a generous roof space.

The listing is an invitation to investigate. It is not confirmation that your proposal is acceptable.

Establish the existing lawful use, relevant planning history and the route your proposed work would need.

If permission already exists, get the decision, approved drawings and conditions. Check that they relate to the scheme you have priced.

What needs to happen before work starts? What must be delivered before occupation? Is there a time limit or another unresolved requirement?

An attractive drawing does not tell you whether somebody obtained permission, changed the design or complied with the conditions.

You need the documents behind the claim.

### Permitted development still has requirements

Permitted development can be a useful route. It does not mean you can do anything that looks similar to a project you saw online.

The particular right has limits and conditions. Some routes require prior approval. Local restrictions and the property's circumstances can affect what is available. England and Wales have different rules.

Establish which route applies to your proposal and what evidence or applications are needed.

If your plan relies on permitted development, ask the person advising you to identify the actual right and explain how the scheme meets its requirements.

“Should be PD” is a research note. It is not the end of the work.

The same goes for an extension you believe is straightforward. Check the actual dimensions, position and restrictions before including its finished value in the appraisal.

### Permission, construction and licensing are different questions

This is where a project can look further ahead than it is.

Planning deals with whether the proposed development or use is acceptable in planning terms. Building regulations deal with standards for the building work. HMO licensing is another process again.

One approval does not answer all the other questions.

You might have a planning route but a design that needs changing to meet construction or fire-safety requirements. You might have an existing HMO licence but still need to establish the planning position for the use you intend.

For an HMO, check the intended occupation, room sizes, shared facilities, fire arrangements and the relevant local requirements as part of a coordinated design.

Do not multiply a room rent by seven because you can draw seven rectangles on a floor plan.

**The number of rooms you can draw is not necessarily the number you can operate.**

### Article 4: understand what the restriction actually covers

We secured an HMO in an Article 4 area in a building that had been a nightclub and offices.

That can sound contradictory if you treat “Article 4” as shorthand for “no HMOs”.

It is not about knowing somebody in the planning department. It is about knowing the rules, the building's existing use and what the particular restriction covers.

In our case, the distinction was that we were not taking an existing family house and turning it into shared accommodation. We were proposing a new use for a former commercial building.

That gave us a different planning proposition, and we secured permission.

Do not turn that example into another blanket rule. A former office or nightclub does not automatically qualify as an HMO. The proposal still has to be assessed on its own merits and against the relevant policies.

But do not reject a building just because somebody says “Article 4” either.

**Read what the restriction actually does. Then assess the proposal you are actually making.**

### Find the requirement that changes the layout

A design change can alter the number of units, the cost and the end value. When that happens, go back to the appraisal. Paying for useful feasibility work can be much cheaper than buying an outcome that only exists in your sketch.

### Reading: the planning route took more than one attempt

Our ten-flat commercial conversion in Reading shows how much work can sit behind the words “permitted development”.

We intended to use PD, and that remains the route for the conversion. But our first prior-approval application failed because of noise from a neighbouring unit.

We had to revise the proposal to address noise protection.

The second application failed on the light and ventilation arrangements. That meant another change: a separate planning application for alterations to the building, including additional rooflights, followed by the PD application based on the revised building.

It was not a case of drawing ten flats, submitting a form and getting on with the build.

We had to understand the objections, establish a route to address them and keep moving the proposal forward.

The purchase price is £525,000. The projected gross development value is £2.1 million once the scheme is completed.

That is a substantial opportunity, but the difference between those two figures is not the profit. Construction, professional fees, finance and all the other costs sit between them.

And the planning process has already taken time and money. While we worked through the applications and changes, holding costs continued.

This is the practical reason to understand the planning route before buying. You need to know what will be assessed, what evidence is required and whether a redesign could affect the value or viability of the scheme.

A refusal does not always mean the opportunity is finished. It can identify a problem you can solve. But the next application costs time, and time can cost money.

**If your appraisal allows for the building work but not the journey to getting that work approved, part of the project is missing.**

### Can you physically get the work done?

A legal right to access a property is important. So is the practical ability to use the access for your works.

Where will materials go? How will waste leave? Can deliveries reach the site? Is there room for scaffolding? Will you need somebody else's agreement to use land or carry out part of the work?

A house in the middle of a terrace may need a different delivery plan from one with its own wide driveway.

An occupied shop below a conversion adds another set of practical questions. How will you keep it operating safely? What access, separation and working arrangements are needed?

Tell the contractor what the site is actually like. A quote based on easy access can become a different price when everything has to be carried through a narrow route.

Access can change the cost, programme and practicality of the whole job.

### Services: the building has electricity, but is it enough?

Do not assume the services for the existing use will support the proposed one.

More units, bathrooms or cooking facilities can mean different demands on electricity, water and drainage.

Establish the capacity, connection arrangements and any upgrades the scheme needs. Find out who must carry them out, what they cost and how long they could take.

Some of the work may depend on outside organisations. Your builder cannot promise their timetable on their behalf.

Also decide how the finished property will be metered and operated. A design that looks complete can still have an expensive gap in how the occupiers receive and pay for services.

Put those decisions into the scope, budget and programme.

A connection cost found after purchase still has to be paid.

### The finance has to match the whole journey

The question is not simply whether somebody will lend against the purchase.

Can the proposed product accommodate the property's starting condition, intended use and works? What are the conditions for drawing the money? How long is the term? What does the lender need before the next stage or exit?

Then look at the refinance or sale you intend to use to repay it.

A property can be acceptable to one lender and unsuitable for another product. A valuation basis you assumed may not be the one used. A change in the scheme can change the finance options.

Give your broker the plan and the awkward details at the start.

The short lease, unusual title, existing movement report or proposed room arrangement is not something to reveal after everybody has spent money on the wrong application.

You want the finance discussion to expose constraints early enough to act on them.

### Mozarts: a viable project still needs time

Our Mozarts project shows why this matters.

The 26-bedroom HMO went far enough over programme that we needed replacement development finance. At the time of writing, the additional fees and interest were expected to total roughly £50,000 or more.

The project did not need to become worthless for the delay to become expensive.

Running beyond the funding timetable was enough.

So when you assess a development, bring the permissions, design, works, services and finance term into one programme.

What must happen before the builder starts? What depends on an approval, inspection or outside connection? What evidence does the exit lender need?

If those steps are missing from the timetable, they are still waiting for you in real life.

### Put every possible blocker in one place

You do not need an elaborate system to start. You need a record you will actually use.

For each important issue, write the question, the evidence needed, who is dealing with it and when it must be resolved.

| Question | Evidence to obtain | Who helps answer it? |
|---|---|---|
| Can I carry out the intended development or use? | Relevant history, decisions and advice on the proposed route | Planning adviser and legal team |
| Will the scheme meet construction and operating requirements? | Coordinated design, assessments and relevant approvals | Designer and appropriate specialists |
| Do I have the rights and access the plan needs? | Title documents, rights and any necessary agreements | Conveyancer |
| Are services suitable for the finished use? | Capacity checks, upgrade proposals, costs and lead times | Relevant providers and designers |
| Does the finance support the plan and timetable? | Product terms, conditions, net funds and exit requirements | Broker and lender |

An answer can be “yes”, “no” or “not established yet”.

The third answer is useful. It tells you where the next piece of work belongs.

The danger is treating it as “yes” because nobody has objected.

### Resolve it, price it or change the deal

A problem does not always mean walking away.

You may be able to change the design, choose another finance product, negotiate a lower price or pursue a different use.

Trebanos had four obvious issues. We worked through them with the appropriate evidence and arrangements. That was part of the opportunity.

The important distinction is whether the remaining uncertainty fits what you are prepared to take on.

Sometimes the answer is to spend a small amount establishing feasibility. Sometimes your legal team can advise on a purchase structure that addresses an important condition. Sometimes the seller's required timetable leaves you without enough time to answer the question.

Do not confuse pressure to decide with evidence that you should proceed.

If the plan changes, update the appraisal. If the costs change, update the offer ceiling. If the main source of value disappears, reassess the purchase.

You do not owe a property your commitment because you have already spent a week thinking about it.

### Turn your red flags into next actions

Use Property Predator to keep the property analysis and report alongside your research. Carry the unresolved issues into the appraisal rather than leaving them in a separate conversation you may forget.

An analysis can show the effect of a higher cost or different exit value. It cannot grant a permission, create an access right or bind a lender.

Attach the actual evidence when the question is answered.

That gives you a record you can return to: this was the concern, this is what resolved it, and this is how the result changed the deal.

Start with the question that could make the rest of the research unnecessary.

By now, you should have more than an interesting property and an optimistic spreadsheet. You should know what the plan depends on and how far those dependencies have been checked.

But permission, funding and a workable design do not finish a refurbishment.

Somebody still has to organise the work, choose the people, control the spending and notice when the job starts slipping.

**A deal can survive every check so far and still be undone by how it is delivered.**
