# Chapter 2
## Where Should I Look?

Open a property website without a clear search and you can lose an evening looking at houses you were never going to buy.

A cheap terrace in one town. A flat above a shop somewhere else. Then a development site because the agent mentions “potential”.

By bedtime, you have saved twenty listings and learned very little about any of them.

Your buying brief from Chapter One gives you a way out. Use it to choose a small search area, learn what works there and build a steady flow of opportunities.

The aim is to recognise a worthwhile property when it appears, because you understand what you are comparing it with.

### Why we chose Swansea

When we started sourcing properties, I lived in Somerset and my business partner, Meirion, lived in Carmarthen. We needed somewhere we could actually get to and work in.

So we looked up and down the M5 corridor and across the M4. We compared purchase prices with the rents we thought the properties could achieve.

As we moved west past Bridgend, we started finding prices closer to those we associated with northern England. In the opportunities we were assessing, the relationship between price and rent looked more attractive.

That was what we were looking for: yield.

Some investors put more weight on capital growth. They buy because they expect the property to become worth more over time. My preference is cash flow. I want to understand what the property can leave each month after its costs, rather than need a future price rise to make the purchase worthwhile.

A hoped-for increase in value does not pay this month's bills.

Yield helps you compare the rent with the purchase price. Cash flow takes you further: what is left after finance, management, repairs, empty periods and the other costs? A high headline yield can still leave very little in your bank account. We will work through that properly in Chapter Five.

Swansea gave us a market we could reach, with prices and rents worth investigating. It is Wales's second-largest city, with a range of employers and reasons for people to live there. We could research different neighbourhoods without having to build our business six hours from home.

Then we learned it. We learned the streets, the local price differences and which properties might suit the investors we were speaking to.

The lesson for you is the route we took to that decision. Start with your goal. Compare areas you can realistically operate in. Find out whether the local prices, demand and costs support your approach.

You may end up choosing somewhere completely different. What matters is being able to explain why.

### Find out what keeps the area working

A property needs people who want to use it and can afford the price you need.

Start with the reasons people live in, work in or visit the area. Look at employers, transport, shops, schools and other services relevant to your intended tenant or buyer.

Then ask how dependent the area is on one source of demand. A busy local employer can support a rental market. Heavy reliance on that employer is also something to understand.

Look at what is changing. New building, investment and improved transport may matter. Find out what has actually been delivered, what has been approved and what is still an idea.

An agent mentioning a proposed station does not put trains on the platform.

Record the source and date of anything you use. Treat future improvements as possibilities to investigate, rather than income the property already earns.

We will test demand in detail in the next chapter. For now, you are deciding which areas deserve that work.

### A postcode is too big to buy on

Swansea taught me how quickly a market can change.

Townhill and Cockett sit next to each other. In the places we were looking, you could drive a very short distance, cross a road, and find a noticeable change in the housing, the feel of the street and the prices people would pay.

You might be a stone's throw away. You could still be looking at a different local market.

That is why “a three-bedroom house nearby sold for this much” is only the beginning of a comparison. Nearby in which direction? On which street? With what surroundings, condition and access?

You need to know whether your buyer or tenant would see those properties as real alternatives.

The same question is worth asking in any city. An area average can hide the road that people particularly want and the road they tend to avoid. Do not assume that a map circle describes one market.

My old sourcing checklist included a simple instruction: **walk it and talk it**.

Walk the streets. Visit at different times where practical. Notice the condition of neighbouring buildings, traffic, vacant shops, access, parking and how the street feels in use.

Talk to local agents and people who know the area. Ask specific questions. “Is it a good area?” often gets you a vague answer. Try: “What changes when I cross this road?” Or: “Which streets do applicants ask for, and which properties take longer to let?”

Local opinion is another piece of evidence. Check it against what you can observe and the market records you can find.

The more you learn, the more useful your comparisons become. You stop seeing twenty similar houses and start seeing why they are priced differently.

### Give your search some boundaries

Turn your brief into a set of buying criteria. These are the rules that tell you which listings deserve attention.

Keep them short enough to use.

| Criterion | An illustrative starting point |
|---|---|
| Strategy | A single let I can hold after refurbishment |
| Area | Two neighbourhoods I can visit regularly |
| Property | A house suited to the intended local rental market |
| Work | A manageable refurbishment, with specialist costs investigated |
| Money | Total cash needed must fit my budget and leave my contingency intact |
| Decision | Investigate only where credible rent and cost evidence could support the plan |

These are example criteria, not a recommendation to buy that type of property. Yours should follow your circumstances and the demand you establish.

Also write down what you will exclude. If you cannot fund structural work, a property with suspected movement needs an early investigation before you spend weeks planning its new kitchen.

Your criteria should save time. They should not become excuses to ignore an important problem because the rest of the listing looks attractive.

Property portals are useful. Set up focused alerts and record what comes on, what changes price and what returns to the market.

### Get to know the agents other buyers overlook

Agents are paid to achieve a sale for their client. You are trying to buy at a price that works for your plan. Understand both sides of that relationship.

I like speaking to auction houses and smaller agents that do not always get the biggest, brightest, easiest-to-sell properties. They may handle the tired house, the awkward layout or the property that needs someone willing to do the work.

Those are worth a conversation.

A small agent does not automatically mean a cheap property. An auction does not automatically mean a bargain. But both can put you in front of opportunities that deserve a closer look.

And remember how the business works. For many agents, completed sales contribute to commission and targets. There are wages, mortgages and family bills behind that desk. A buyer who can proceed, communicate clearly and complete is valuable.

You become useful by doing what you say you will do. Turn up. Give a clear answer. Explain an offer. Have your funding position straight. If you cannot proceed, say so early.

Do not claim to be a cash buyer if you still need someone to agree the money. Credibility takes longer to build than it takes to lose.

When an agent sends something unsuitable, give useful feedback. “Too expensive” tells them little. “Once I include the works and purchase costs, the rent does not support my plan at that price” explains the problem.

Help them understand which properties you can genuinely buy. Over time, you want to be someone they think of when the right opportunity arrives.

### Build a route beyond the listings

You can also investigate referrals, local networks and direct approaches to owners. You do not have to wait for every opportunity to arrive on a portal.

Property Predator includes off-market tools to help identify properties worth investigating, find ownership information and prepare letter campaigns. For example, you can look for signals that a landlord may face a decision about spending money on a property or selling it, then investigate whether there is a conversation worth having.

A signal is a reason to research. It does not tell you that the owner needs to sell or wants an approach.

Where the tools and ownership details are available, you can set up a three-letter campaign through the platform. That gives you a structured way to introduce yourself and follow up, rather than sending one letter and forgetting about it. Check the current plan, coverage and sending charges before starting; the free extension and these wider tools are different parts of the offer.

Keep approaches clear and respectful. Explain who you are and why you are writing, and respect a request to stop.

Finding a property off-market does not make it a bargain. Finding it online does not make it a bad deal. Assess the opportunity underneath the introduction.

Each route also changes the work you must do. An auction deadline, for example, brings the legal and funding checks forward. If someone else sources the property, include their fee and independently check the information you are relying on.

### Look for a reason to investigate

A listing might catch my attention because it has been on the market for a long time, needs work, has an awkward layout or contains space that appears underused.

That gives me questions, rather than an instant buying decision.

Why has it not sold? What work is needed? Can the layout be improved? Is the proposed extra use permitted and practical?

The opportunity may be something other buyers cannot be bothered to investigate. The problem may also be exactly why they walked away.

| What catches your eye | The next question |
|---|---|
| Long time on the market | What has stopped a sale, and has anything changed? |
| Low asking price | Low compared with which genuinely similar properties? |
| Poor presentation | Is this cosmetic work or a sign of more serious defects? |
| Unused rooms or land | Can that space be used as proposed, and at what cost? |
| “High yield” in the advert | Which rent and costs produced that figure? |

I look for value I can understand and a practical route to improving the result. Enthusiasm about “potential” is not enough to price the work.

### Keep a useful shortlist

Give every promising property a simple record:

- The address, link, asking price and date you found it.
- Why it might fit your buying brief.
- The evidence you have so far.
- The questions that could change the deal.
- Your next action and when you will do it.

Use Property Predator to generate the property analysis and report, then bring the key findings into your shortlist. Keep the source evidence beside the assumptions so you can see what is known and what still needs checking.

The companion research pack gives you a printable way to compare the results. The purpose is to make the next decision easier: which property deserves more of your time?

At this stage, mark a property as **reject**, **investigate** or **ready to view**. You are choosing where to spend your next hour, not committing to a purchase.

### Your next move

Choose two areas. Record ten broadly comparable listings in each, then speak to two local agents and visit the streets that look relevant.

Twenty listings will not prove demand or establish a valuation. They give you a starting point for noticing differences and asking better questions.

From that work, shortlist three properties. Write one clear reason to investigate each and the biggest unanswered question.

You should now be able to explain why you are looking in those areas, why those streets matter and why those three properties deserve another hour of your time.

But there is a trap here. You can find a house you would happily live in, at a price you like, in an area you understand, and still struggle to rent or sell it at the figure your plan needs.

**Because you are not necessarily the customer.**

Before we put money against the opportunity, we need to know who will want the finished property and what evidence says they will pay for it. That is where we go next.
