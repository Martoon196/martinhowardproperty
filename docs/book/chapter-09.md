# Chapter 9
## Should I Buy It—and at What Price?

The agent calls.

“Are you putting an offer in?”

You have looked at the house, spoken to people, collected figures and started imagining what you could do with it.

Now you need an answer.

This is where all the work in this book comes together.

You do not need to know everything about every property. You need enough reliable information to decide what to do next—and enough discipline to recognise what you have not established.

Sometimes the answer is an offer. Sometimes it is another question. Sometimes it is no.

**The aim is to buy a deal you understand at a price that works.**

### Bring the deal onto one page

Before making the decision, write a short explanation of the opportunity.

What are you buying? What will you do with it? Where does the value come from? How will you fund it? How will you finish or exit it?

Then put the main figures beside that explanation.

If you need a long conversation to explain why the numbers almost work, stop and simplify the plan.

You should be able to say:

“I intend to buy this property for this purpose. These are the supported income or sale assumptions. This is the cost, this is the cash needed, and these are the issues still to resolve.”

That is a much stronger position than “it looks cheap”.

| Decision | What you should be able to explain |
|---|---|
| Fit | Why the project suits your goal, money, time and team |
| Market | What evidence supports the area, price and intended income |
| Property | What you noticed, what has been investigated and what remains |
| Numbers | The full costs, contingency, expected result and weaker outcomes |
| Feasibility | What permissions, rights, services and funding the plan needs |
| Delivery | Who will carry out and control the work |
| Exit | How the money returns, when and what the backup needs |

A gap in the table is not automatically a reason to reject the property. It is a reason to identify the next action.

### Separate what you know from what you hope

A written contractor quote is evidence of a price for the work it covers.

A comparable sale is evidence of a transaction, with differences you still need to consider.

A broker's initial view is useful guidance. It is not the same as a lender's completed underwriting and offer.

Your confidence should reflect those distinctions.

Mark the important assumptions:

- **Established:** supported sufficiently for the decision you are making.
- **To investigate:** plausible, but still needs evidence.
- **Not workable:** an answer that currently defeats the plan.

The stage matters. You can make an initial offer while investigations remain, provided the offer is framed appropriately and you understand what commitment you are making.

But do not carry the same unanswered question through to a binding commitment because everybody seems keen.

An auction bid and an ordinary negotiated offer may put you in very different positions. Have your legal team explain the actual commitment and timetable before you act.

**Know what you are committing to at the point you commit.**

### Find the assumption doing the heavy lifting

Every deal has numbers that matter more than the others.

Perhaps a small change in the sale price removes most of the profit. Perhaps the refinance only works with a particular valuation basis. Perhaps one additional letting room makes the income look acceptable.

Find that assumption.

What evidence supports it? What happens if it is wrong? Can you afford the alternative?

At Reading, the ten-flat plan needed a planning route that addressed noise and the building's light and ventilation arrangements. A drawing and a projected end value did not settle those questions.

At Trebanos, the apparent problems needed the title arrangements, structural evidence, knotweed management and site-specific flood position worked through.

The opportunity was not in pretending the awkward parts did not exist. It was in understanding what to do about them.

If a single unconfirmed assumption carries the whole profit, make answering it your priority.

### Set your price before the negotiation sets it for you

Chapter Five showed the basic approach:

Supported exit value, less the full costs, less the return you require, gives you a starting point for the price you can pay.

For a rental, work through the sustainable cash flow, funding and cash left invested.

Then test the weaker outcomes.

Your maximum should reflect your actual position. A buyer with a trusted local team, spare capital and relevant experience may be able to take on a project that does not suit you.

That does not make their offer right for your circumstances.

Do not increase your price simply because someone says another buyer will pay more.

Perhaps they will.

They may have a different plan. They may know something you do not. They may also be making a mistake.

Your job is to establish whether your plan works at your price.

### Make the offer easy to understand

Explain what you are offering and the basis on which you can proceed.

Be clear about the buyer, funding position, timetable and any investigations or conditions that matter. Get the legal wording and purchase structure right for the transaction.

If your finance is still subject to valuation and underwriting, say so. If you need an investor to agree the money, do not describe it as ready cash.

An offer is more useful when the agent can explain it to the seller without guessing.

You can support your price with the main evidence: works needed, comparable values, funding constraints or another relevant factor.

You do not need to give a theatrical performance about how terrible the house is.

Remember the relationship from Chapter Two. A reliable buyer who communicates clearly can be valuable to an agent and seller.

On Clare Street, the seller needed a quick sale. The opportunity came from a cash purchase at £95,000, followed by a completed resale at £120,000 about three months later.

Price mattered. So did the ability to proceed.

Only promise the timetable and certainty you can genuinely offer.

### Negotiate the deal, not just the headline

Price is important. So are the costs and obligations around it.

What is included? When is completion? What must happen first? What does each side need?

A lower purchase price may be less attractive if the terms create a financing problem or force you to commit before an essential question is answered.

Conversely, a purchase structure your legal team has properly arranged may make an otherwise difficult opportunity workable.

Bring the agreed terms back into the appraisal and cash plan. Do not assess a revised price using costs and dates from the old proposal.

You are negotiating a complete transaction.

### Do not spend the contingency to win the house

This is an easy mistake.

Your appraisal works at one price. The seller wants more.

You decide the refurbishment can be cheaper. Then the contingency becomes “a bit generous”. Then you use the highest rent or sale figure you heard.

Nothing about the property has improved. You have changed the assumptions to afford it.

If better evidence supports a change, use it. If you are deleting allowances because you want to win, recognise what you are doing.

A contingency is there for the project. It is not an extra bidding fund.

**Winning the negotiation is not the same as buying well.**

### Three useful decisions

You do not have to force every property into buy or reject immediately.

There are three practical next steps.

**Investigate.** The opportunity could work, but an unanswered question matters. Name the evidence needed, who will obtain it and what it will cost.

**Negotiate.** You understand enough to propose a price and terms that fit the plan, with the appropriate investigations and protections for that stage.

**Walk away.** The established position does not meet your requirements, or the unresolved uncertainty is more than you are willing or able to take on.

Each is a useful result.

Walking away before committing can save the capital and time you need for a better purchase.

Investigating properly can stop you rejecting the sort of property other buyers misunderstand.

The skill is choosing the right next step.

### Walking away is easier when you have written the rule

Your buying brief included a rejection rule.

Use it.

If the project needs more cash than you can provide, resolve the funding or stop. If the intended use is not workable, reassess the actual alternative. If the seller's price leaves too little return for the commitment, leave the offer where the evidence supports it.

It can be frustrating after viewings, calls and professional fees.

But money already spent does not make the next pound sensible.

Think about the next commitment on its own merits. What do you know now, and would you choose the deal on that basis today?

If you need the property to rescue the time you have spent looking at it, step back.

### Accepted does not mean finished

An accepted offer starts another stage.

Keep checking the plan as the survey, legal work, finance and quotations develop. New evidence may change what you are willing to pay or whether you proceed.

Keep the dates under review too.

Number 8 Vivian Street took us eight months to purchase. A timetable can move a long way from what you expected at the first viewing.

Then remember the bridge example: six months of refurbishment, three months to find a buyer and four months for the sale to complete is thirteen months.

A twelve-month facility would not cover that sequence.

The price, works, finance and exit have to keep fitting together as the project changes.

Do not stop appraising the deal because the agent has put “sold subject to contract” on the listing.

### Your final buying decision

Before the next meaningful commitment, answer these questions in writing:

1. **Why this property?** Identify the opportunity and how it serves your goal.
2. **What supports the income or exit value?** Record the evidence and important differences.
3. **What am I taking on?** Summarise the condition, title and other material issues.
4. **What will it really cost?** Include buying, works, funding, holding, exit and contingency.
5. **Can I fund it through to the next stage?** Check cash timing as well as total borrowing.
6. **Can the plan be carried out?** Identify permissions, rights, requirements and remaining dependencies.
7. **Who will deliver it?** Confirm the scope, team and management arrangements.
8. **How do I get out?** Show the main route, net result, timetable and workable backup.
9. **What is my maximum price?** Use the appraisal and your requirements.
10. **What would make me stop?** Name the answer or change that defeats the plan.

If an answer is missing, write the next action rather than filling the gap with confidence.

Bring the buying brief, search pack, comparison evidence, viewing checks and appraisal together. You have now built a decision record for an actual property.

That is the outcome this book was designed to give you.

### The LNW: the cost of a plan that did not move forward

At 71 Sterry Road in Swansea, we bought a former pub known as the London and North Western—the LNW.

We bought from a developer. There had been an approval for flats that had lapsed, and we wanted to pursue an HMO scheme.

We went through pre-application discussions. Our understanding of the feedback was that particular elements would be acceptable, and we developed the application on that basis.

The application was then refused, including on matters we believed the earlier discussions had indicated would be acceptable.

That was deeply frustrating. We had spent time and money trying to establish a route, then found ourselves having to work through further objections and requirements.

In our experience, communication and the planning timetable were difficult. There were repeated requests for extensions to the determination period. The building remained empty for around three years, costing us money while the process dragged on.

Then it suffered a serious fire. We were left dealing with a major insurance situation as well as the development problems.

Those are separate events, but together they show how far a project can move from the original plan.

The lesson is not that you can predict a fire. It is that the period before development starts is still part of the investment. An empty building has holding costs, security needs and insurance arrangements that must remain suitable as circumstances change.

For the next purchase, ask what happens if the planning stage takes much longer than expected. How long can you fund ownership? What are the alternatives if the intended scheme is refused? What costs and responsibilities continue while the building waits?

Also recognise what pre-application advice gives you. It can help identify a route and expose objections; it is not the final permission. Keep the written advice and have your planning team identify the assumptions and unresolved issues behind it.

Local experience matters too. Speak to people who have taken comparable applications through that authority. Ask about actual timescales, recurring objections and communication. Use that experience to inform the programme, rather than assuming the stated target will be your completion date.

**A property can cost you money for a long time before a builder starts work.**

### Gowerton: the purchase we did not make

Another building in Gowerton looked as though it could become around six flats.

It was a former shop with office space at the back. It was empty and needed a new use.

That sounds like the sort of opportunity worth investigating.

But the site was near a river with a history of flooding. Through the pre-application process, we established that the flood-risk position and the requirements involving Natural Resources Wales presented a major obstacle to our residential proposal.

We explored ways of dealing with it, including arrangements for refuge upstairs. We did not establish an acceptable solution for the scheme we wanted to deliver.

So we did not buy it.

That is an important result. We had found the question that could stop the deal before taking on the purchase and its holding costs.

It would have been easy to say: “It is an empty building. Surely somebody will let us turn it into something useful.”

But the fact that a building would benefit from investment does not establish that your proposed use can be approved.

Nor does being able to imagine an emergency arrangement establish that it will satisfy the relevant flood-safety requirements.

We were investigating a particular proposal on a particular site. We did not find a workable route for it. That was enough to make our decision, without needing to prove that nobody could ever find another use.

The contrast with the LNW matters.

On one project, we owned the building while the planning difficulties and costs continued. On the other, the research told us to stop before buying.

**Sometimes the return on your research is the property you do not buy.**

### Before you buy

You do not have to buy a perfect property.

Some of our opportunities have looked far from perfect. A messy garden. A seller under time pressure. A pair of houses with several issues other buyers did not want to deal with.

The work is finding the difference between a problem you can resolve and a problem you cannot afford.

That takes curiosity, evidence, sensible numbers and the willingness to make a decision.

You will not remove every uncertainty. Property does not work like that. You can understand more of what you are taking on and leave yourself room to deal with it.

So take the next property that interests you and work through the process.

Ask the questions. Get the evidence. Run the numbers.

Then make the offer that fits—or leave the property for somebody else.

**You make the commitment before you own the outcome. Make it a commitment you can explain.**
