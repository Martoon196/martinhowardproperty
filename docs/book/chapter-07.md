# Chapter 7
## Can I Deliver It?

Our first single let nearly taught us a very expensive lesson.

It was on Danygraig Road in Swansea. We had found it for our first investor partner, Chris, and the project had plenty going for it.

The garden was a huge job. The house needed work. We had an opportunity to improve it and get it operating as a rental.

Then we got the wrong builder.

He was a plumber by trade. The work was a mess. He brought in young painters he had found through Facebook, and paint ended up where it had no business being, including on the floors.

But poor decorating was nowhere near the worst of it.

During cleaning, the cooker hood came away because it had been fixed so badly. That led us to discover open gas pipework behind the new electric cooker.

Work we had believed was finished concealed a serious safety problem.

We dealt with the issues, got the property properly finished and operating, and the investor was happy with the outcome.

But getting there was a hell of a start.

**The fact that a builder says “finished” does not mean the job is finished.**

### Buying the opportunity is only the beginning

By now, you have assessed the property, investigated the important issues and built an appraisal.

Delivery is where those assumptions meet people, materials, invoices and time.

Somebody has to decide exactly what will be done. Somebody has to check the work. Somebody has to notice that the electrician is waiting for a decision, the kitchen is late or the builder's next payment does not match the progress on site.

If nobody owns those jobs, you own the consequences.

You can manage the project yourself or pay somebody to manage it. Either way, decide who is responsible before you start.

A profitable spreadsheet does not organise a refurbishment.

### Write the finish line first

What does “done” mean for this property?

For a single let, it should mean a property ready to be let and occupied, with the relevant checks, documents and practical arrangements in place.

For a flip, it means a property ready for the intended buyer, with the completed work and supporting paperwork you will need for the sale.

For a conversion or HMO, the finish line may include approvals, inspections, service connections and operating requirements that need to be coordinated throughout the job.

Write down the outcome, rather than relying on “a decent refurb”.

Which rooms are changing? What is being repaired or replaced? What finish are you paying for? What documents and inspections belong to the work?

Then ask who checks each part and what evidence shows it is complete.

If you cannot describe the finish line, you will struggle to agree whether you have reached it.

### On a small refurb, you may be leading the trades yourself

For a small house refurbishment, employing a main building contractor may cost more than the deal can comfortably support.

A practical alternative is to appoint the individual trades: a plasterer, electrician, plumber, decorator and whoever else the scope needs.

Local one- or two-person firms can be a good fit. You are paying for the work you need and taking responsibility for coordinating it yourself.

That makes you the leader of the build team.

You decide the sequence, arrange access, order what you have agreed to supply and make sure one person's work does not prevent the next person doing theirs.

The plasterer needs the preceding work ready. The decorator needs suitable surfaces. The electrician and plumber need the right access at the right stage.

Each trade is responsible for its agreed work and the testing or certification applicable to that work. Establish the relevant qualifications, registrations and sign-off arrangements before appointing them. A promised certificate is not a substitute for checking the person or the work, and not every plumbing job has the same certification requirements.

Then manage the practical standard. Do they turn up? Is the finish acceptable? Do they clear waste and leave the site safe and workable for the next trade?

A plasterer who leaves poor work may need to put it right or be replaced. A qualified trade can still leave a mess, miss an appointment or hold up another part of the job.

Agree who makes good around each installation, who clears what and what “finished” includes. Otherwise, the small gaps between people's scopes become jobs you still need to pay for.

**You can save a contractor's management margin by managing the trades yourself. You still have to do the management.**

### Give builders the same job to price

Three quotes are only useful if they cover broadly the same work.

One builder includes waste removal. Another assumes you are supplying the skip. One includes decorating. Another stops at plastering. One has allowed for a full electrical scope; another has written “electrics as required”.

The cheapest total may simply contain the fewest answers.

Prepare a scope of works. It does not need to be fancy. It needs to be clear enough that the people pricing it understand the job.

For a straightforward refurbishment, work through it room by room and trade by trade.

| Part of the scope | What to make clear |
|---|---|
| Preparation | Strip-out, protection, access, waste and what stays |
| Repairs | The defect, intended work and any investigation still required |
| Services | The agreed electrical, heating, plumbing and ventilation work |
| Finishes | Materials, fittings, decorating and the standard expected |
| Completion | Testing, relevant certification, inspections, snagging and handover |
| Price assumptions | VAT, exclusions, allowances and who supplies what |

For more involved work, get the drawings, specifications and specialist design coordinated before asking someone to promise a reliable price.

A builder cannot accurately price decisions you have not made.

### Choose evidence over confidence

Plenty of people can stand in a house and sound convincing.

Ask what similar work they have actually completed. Look at finished jobs where possible. Speak to previous clients and ask about the difficult parts.

Did the price change? Why? How were variations handled? Did they return to finish the snags? Was the communication useful when the job slipped?

Ask who will actually be on your site and who supervises them.

The person selling you the job may not be the person doing it. Subcontracting can work perfectly well, but you need to know who controls the standard and coordination.

Check relevant experience, insurance and the qualifications or registrations needed for the work concerned.

Do not confuse “a builder I like” with “a builder whose work and arrangements I have checked”.

Our Danygraig Road experience taught us that the painful way.

### Agree more than a price

You need a written agreement that suits the work.

It should set out the scope, price or pricing basis, payment stages, programme, responsibilities and how changes or problems will be handled.

For a larger or more complex project, get appropriate help choosing and putting the agreement together.

Useful questions include:

- What exactly is included and excluded?
- Who buys the materials, and when?
- What must be completed before each payment?
- Who checks the work?
- What happens when an unexpected issue is uncovered?
- How are changes priced and approved?
- What happens if the programme slips?
- What is required for handover and putting defects right?

A written agreement does not make a bad contractor good. It gives both sides a clearer job to deliver and a record to refer to.

The time to agree the difficult bits is before they become an argument.

### Pay against progress you can establish

A request for money tells you the contractor wants paying. It does not tell you the corresponding work has been completed properly.

Agree sensible payment stages and check them.

Some materials and specialist orders may need deposits. Understand what you are paying for, the supplier arrangements and the evidence behind the request.

Avoid letting payments get far ahead of the value properly delivered without understanding and accepting the exposure.

Larger projects commonly have a project manager or project-managing surveyor, with scheduled payments through the build and progress assessed against the agreed work. The lender may also appoint a monitoring surveyor. Be clear about each person's role: monitoring the lender's security is not automatically the same as managing your contractor.

On a smaller job, you still need a reliable way to check each trade's progress and invoice.

A few photographs can be useful. They may not show concealed work or answer whether something has been installed correctly.

If you do not have the knowledge to assess a stage, arrange the help before the invoice arrives.

**Checking a payment is part of managing the job. It is not an insult to the builder.**

### Match contractor payments to lender drawdowns

Where bridging or development finance includes a works facility, the lender may release the build funds in stages, often called tranches.

Those releases are commonly linked to evidence of eligible work completed and expenditure incurred. Depending on the lender and facility, that may involve a monitoring surveyor's inspection. Some smaller facilities may accept photographs, invoices and progress updates.

Read the actual drawdown conditions. Not every bridging loan includes build funding, and evidence requirements differ.

If you must pay for a stage before the lender releases the corresponding money, you need enough working capital to bridge that gap. Allow for inspection, processing and any deductions as well.

Your trade's payment date and the lender's release date are two different dates until you have planned them together.

Put both into the cash-flow forecast. A facility can have enough money available overall while you still run short between stages.

**An agreed loan amount is not the same as cash available today.**

### Hidden work needs attention before it disappears

Fresh plaster and new kitchen units can hide work you will struggle to inspect later.

Identify the stages when inspection, testing or evidence is needed before something is covered.

That might involve your building-control arrangements, the relevant trade or another competent person, depending on the job.

Ask for useful records and photographs of work that will become concealed. Keep the documents in the project file.

Photographs do not replace the relevant checks. They help build the record.

At Danygraig Road, the danger was hidden behind something new. The finish looked like progress while the underlying work had not been made safe.

That is why “we will check everything at the end” is a weak delivery plan.

### Changes need a price and a decision

Something unexpected turns up.

The builder calls. There is more work. You want the job moving, so you say yes.

Then another change. Then a better fitting. Then a layout adjustment.

By the time the invoices arrive, the original price is no longer a useful guide.

Record each change:

1. What has changed and why?
2. What will it cost, including any relevant VAT?
3. What does it do to the programme?
4. Who has approved it?

For urgent work needed to make something safe, deal with the immediate risk and record the decision promptly. For ordinary changes, establish the effect before authorising them.

Keep a running forecast of the cost to finish.

The question is not only how much you have spent. It is how much remains committed and what is still needed to complete.

A project can look comfortably within budget because the largest remaining invoices have not arrived.

### Build a programme that includes the waiting

A programme is more than a completion date.

What happens first? What depends on something else? Which materials need ordering early? When do decisions, inspections and connections need to happen?

A kitchen delivery booked for next week does not help if the room cannot be ready for it. A plasterer arriving before the necessary services work is finished may create rework.

Ask the people doing the job to explain the sequence and the dependencies.

Then include the things outside the builder's direct control: approvals, provider appointments, lender inspections and other relevant steps.

At Mozarts, time overrun brought replacement development finance and roughly £50,000-plus of expected additional fees and interest into the project.

That is why a missed date can matter beyond the inconvenience.

Update the programme when reality changes. An old completion date left on a spreadsheet does not protect your finance term.

### Keep the project moving with clear decisions

You do not need to spend every day standing behind a builder.

You do need a rhythm for checking the project and making decisions.

Agree who reports progress, how often and what the update includes.

I want to know:

- What was completed?
- What is happening next?
- What is waiting for a decision, material or another trade?
- What has changed in the cost to finish?
- What has changed in the likely completion date?

A useful site visit follows those questions. It is more than walking around saying the walls look nice.

Keep a short action list, with a person and date against each item. Close the loop when the action is done.

Small unanswered questions can stop a surprising amount of work.

### If the job starts going wrong

Do not keep paying and hope the next week will be different.

Establish what has been completed, what is defective, what is outstanding and what money remains committed.

If there is a safety concern, stop the affected activity or use and bring in the appropriate competent person. Do not improvise with gas, electrics or structural work.

Tell the contractor clearly what the concern is and what needs to happen. Keep the record factual.

If the working relationship is breaking down, get advice on the agreement and your options before making decisions that could create another dispute.

Replacing a contractor can bring delay, extra cost and disagreement about the work already done. That does not make staying with the wrong arrangement sensible. It means the decision needs managing.

At Danygraig Road, delivering for our investor meant working through the problems. Passing on the builder's excuses would not have finished the house.

You can delegate work. You still need to make sure the project is being controlled.

### Finish properly

Practical completion, payment and readiness to let or sell are not always the same event.

Walk the work against the agreed scope. Record outstanding defects and incomplete items. Agree who will resolve them and when.

Collect the relevant certificates, approvals, warranties, instructions and other handover documents for the work undertaken.

Check the practical details too. Keys. Locks. Heating controls. Water. Appliances. Cleaning. Waste cleared. The things the next person using the building will notice immediately.

Confirm that the property is ready for its intended use.

A missing document can slow a refinance or sale. An unfinished repair can become the first problem for your tenant. A dangerous defect is more serious than either.

Do not let “nearly there” drift into nobody owning the last ten jobs.

### What the first project taught us

Danygraig Road did work out. We dealt with the mess, finished the property and got it running. Our investor was happy.

The useful lesson is not that a project needs to go badly before you can learn.

It is that finding a good opportunity and delivering a good project are separate skills.

Build a clear scope. Check the people. Agree the terms. Control the changes. Check progress before payment. Get the work and handover properly completed.

Those steps may sound ordinary.

They become very interesting when the alternative is paying twice.

### Your delivery test

Before committing to a project, answer these questions:

- Can I describe the finished result and the work needed to get there?
- Do the quotes cover the same scope, with exclusions understood?
- Have I checked who will do and supervise the work?
- Are responsibilities, payments and changes agreed?
- Does the programme include the important dependencies?
- Can I fund the work, contingency and a realistic delay?
- Who checks the work and collects the completion evidence?

Use **Example 10: Plan the delivery** as the companion topic reference at **martinhowardproperty.co.uk/book/companion/**.

Keep the scope, quotes, programme, changes and outstanding actions with your property record. Update the appraisal as the project develops so you can see the effect on the result.

You now have a plan for buying and delivering the property.

But the final question is not whether the builder can finish.

It is whether you can sell, refinance or hold on the terms your deal needs—and what you will do if that first route disappoints.

**A finished property is not always a finished deal.**
