## Martin's four viewing checks
These are a first screen, not a complete survey, conveyancing process or finance assessment.

1. Straight and true: floors, ceilings, window ledges, door frames and roof. Inspect roof lines for slope across the building and bows/dips along them. Visible irregularity prompts investigation; appearance alone establishes neither active movement nor structural soundness.
2. Japanese knotweed: identify suspected growth professionally, establish extent including adjacent land, obtain a costed management approach and confirm lender/insurer requirements. No universal claim that it is easy or always mortgageable. Martin intends a specialist-supported reader training pack.
3. Title and tenure: short leases, flying freeholds and creeping freeholds. Flying = over another freehold; creeping = below another freehold. Check exact demise, support/shelter/access/repair rights, covenants and lender acceptability with the conveyancer. Do not use “floating freehold” as the counterpart.
4. Flood risk: Martin reports buying properties flagged by maps but physically uphill from the river. Present this as a prompt to investigate the actual building levels and sources of water. Horizontal distance from a river does not establish vertical separation or eliminate surface-water/runoff/groundwater risk. Obtain appropriate assessment, history and actual insurance terms; lender and insurer decide separately. A mortgage valuation is not a full building survey or flood guarantee.

## Chapter 4 draft insert: Four things I check before the kitchen gets my attention

A new kitchen is easy to notice. So is fresh paint. Neither tells me whether a property is a sensible purchase.

There are four things I look for early: the shape of the building, Japanese knotweed, title problems and flooding. They tell me where I may need more evidence before spending more time or money.

### Is everything straight and true?

Look along the floors, ceilings, window ledges and door frames. Outside, look at the roof from more than one position. A roof can lean across the building. It can also bow or dip along its length.

Record what you see and ask what caused it. An old uneven floor is not proof of a failing building. A straight-looking room is not proof that everything behind the plaster is sound. Where something concerns you, get the right survey and, if needed, structural advice. Price the investigation and any confirmed work into the deal.

**Example 04 — Straight and true:** planned filmed viewing, with marked stills showing where to look. Do not diagnose movement from a photograph alone.

### Is there Japanese knotweed?

Knotweed does not make every property unbuyable. It does give you another job to understand and cost.

Get suspected plants identified, establish the extent and obtain specialist advice on treatment or removal. Ask what the lender requires and which management plan or guarantee it will accept. Growth on neighbouring land can matter too. A cheap quote is little help if it does not address the problem or meet your funding conditions.

**Example 05 — Knotweed:** planned reader pack covering identification, specialist questions, management proposals and funding checks. This is not a DIY treatment instruction.

### What do I own, and what rights come with it?

A room can sit above someone else's freehold: a flying freehold. Part of your freehold can extend beneath theirs: a creeping freehold.

Those names are the start of the enquiry. Who supports the structure? Who repairs it? Can you gain access to do the work? Does the lender accept the arrangement?

Short leases need their own enquiry into the remaining term, costs, restrictions and realistic extension options. Give the paperwork to your conveyancer early. A property you cannot fund or sell as planned needs a different appraisal.

**Example 06 — Above and below:** planned diagram and anonymised title walkthrough. Boundaries on an illustration do not substitute for the actual title and lease.

### What does the flood flag mean for this building?

I have bought properties flagged for flood risk where the house was uphill from the river. That is a reason to check the detail, rather than dismiss the property from a map alone.

It is also a reason to be precise. Fifty metres away is a distance, not a height. Even a house above a river can face water running down the hill or backing up through drainage.

Check the relevant reports, levels and flood history. Get specialist assessment where needed, and obtain insurance terms for the actual use you propose. Check exclusions and excesses as well as the premium. Then confirm the funding position.

The aim is to find out whether a concern is manageable, what it costs and whether the deal still works.

**Example 07 — Map versus property:** planned walkthrough comparing map information, physical levels, specialist findings and insurance terms, using a documented case when supplied.
