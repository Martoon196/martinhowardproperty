# Chapter 8
## How Do I Get Out?

“We will just refinance it.”

“We can always sell.”

“If it does not sell, we will rent it out.”

Those can all be sensible options.

They can also be three ways of avoiding a question you have not answered.

How much money will come back? When will it arrive? What must happen first? And what will you do if the result is lower or later than you planned?

Your exit is how you bring the project into its next stage. That may mean selling, replacing short-term borrowing with a longer-term mortgage, or keeping the property as an operating asset.

Whatever the route, work through it before you buy.

**An exit is a plan with numbers, conditions and a timetable.**

### Decide what the deal is supposed to do

A flip is meant to produce sale proceeds and profit.

A buy-refurbish-refinance project may be intended to leave you with a rental property, manageable debt and some of your original cash returned.

A purchase for long-term ownership may not need an immediate refinance at all. It still needs sustainable funding and a property you can afford to keep.

Be clear about your intended outcome.

If the deal needs £60,000 of your cash and you need £40,000 back for another commitment, that matters. A refinance leaving £45,000 of your money tied up may be a perfectly respectable investment for somebody else. It does not meet your immediate need.

The property does not become suitable just because the headline valuation looks impressive.

### Selling: what will actually come back?

Start with a supported sale estimate, then deduct what sits between the buyer's payment and your bank account.

That includes the borrowing being repaid, finance charges, selling fees, legal costs and other outstanding project liabilities.

Keep the profit calculation alongside the cash calculation. They answer different questions.

Here is an illustrative example.

| At completion | Amount |
|---|---:|
| Sale price | £200,000 |
| Borrowing and accrued finance costs to repay | −£130,000 |
| Selling and legal costs | −£5,000 |
| Cash remaining before other liabilities and tax | £65,000 |

If you put £50,000 of your own money into the project and there are no other costs or liabilities missing from that example, £50,000 of the £65,000 is your original capital coming back.

The remaining £15,000 is profit before tax.

Do not call the whole £65,000 profit. You would be celebrating getting your own money back.

And do not forget invoices that have not yet been paid. They still belong in the deal even if they are settled after the sale.

### An offer is a stage, not the finish line

On Clare Street, we bought for £95,000 cash and completed the resale at £120,000 about three months later.

That completion mattered. It was the point at which the sale proceeds became available, after the relevant costs and deductions.

Before that, a buyer's offer was progress. It was not money you could spend.

A sale can be delayed by the buyer's funding, legal enquiries, a chain or missing information about your property.

You cannot control every part of that process. You can make sure your side is ready.

Get the legal team instructed in good time. Keep the title information, relevant approvals and certificates for the work together. Deal with outstanding questions early.

If you created a leasehold unit or changed the title arrangements, establish what documentation the buyer and their lender will need.

A finished kitchen does not compensate for an unfinished legal pack.

### Price against the market you are selling into

Your appraisal used an expected sale value. By the time the property is ready, check the evidence again.

What has completed? What is competing with you now? What are agents seeing?

You do not have to accept the first low offer. You do need to distinguish an ambitious price from a supported one.

If the property is not attracting interest, investigate why. Is it the presentation, the agent, the price, the condition or a feature buyers keep rejecting?

Then put a cost against waiting.

For an illustrative comparison, suppose holding the property costs £1,500 a month. Waiting another four months costs £6,000 before any other changes.

Holding out for an extra £5,000 would not improve the result on those assumptions.

That does not tell you what any buyer will pay. It tells you to compare the net outcomes rather than defend an asking price out of pride.

**The best price on paper is not always the best result in your bank account.**

### Refinancing: start with the net loan

A valuation is a starting point for a lending calculation. It is not a promise of a particular advance.

The lender still needs to accept the borrower, property, use, income and product requirements. The amount available may be constrained by more than the headline loan-to-value.

Then the gross facility and the money available to you may differ because of fees, retained interest or other deductions.

Ask your broker to show the net position.

Using another illustration:

| Refinance calculation | Amount |
|---|---:|
| Valuation | £200,000 |
| Illustrative loan at 75% of value | £150,000 |
| Existing borrowing to repay | −£110,000 |
| Refinance fees and costs | −£4,000 |
| Net cash released | £36,000 |

Suppose you had invested £50,000 of your own cash in the project.

That refinance returns £36,000 and leaves £14,000 invested, before allowing for anything missing from the illustration.

Now ask whether the property works with that £14,000 left in, and whether you can afford to leave it there.

“Most of my money back” may be a good outcome. It may also be too vague to plan the next purchase around.

### Check the valuation basis before relying on it

This matters particularly with HMOs, conversions and other properties where investors expect the income to support a higher value.

You may have heard that a certain number of rooms will produce a commercial valuation.

Do not build the whole deal on that sentence.

Ask which lender and product you are considering, how the property is likely to be assessed, and what evidence and completed arrangements will be needed.

A room count does not, by itself, establish the valuation basis or the loan available.

If your plan depends on a particular treatment, investigate that dependency before the purchase. Then model an alternative valuation and net advance.

The lender's eventual decision is what funds the exit, rather than the valuation you preferred in your first spreadsheet.

### Trebanos: value, borrowing and cash are three different numbers

Our two Trebanos houses cost £130,000 together. After we finished, they were valued at £130,000 each. Later valuations were £180,000 and £170,000.

That is a strong increase in value.

But there are three separate figures to understand:

- What the properties are valued at.
- What a lender will advance against them.
- What remains after repaying existing finance and paying the refinance costs.

Then there is the debt you will carry afterwards.

Releasing more cash can help you recycle capital. It can also increase monthly finance costs and reduce the cash flow you wanted the property to produce.

Put both results in front of you: cash returned now and the operating position afterwards.

A refinance should support the next stage of ownership, rather than leave you with a property you struggle to hold.

### Do not leave the exit application until the last week

Work backwards from the date the current borrowing must be repaid.

What condition must the property reach? What documents are required? When can the valuation happen? Are there tenancy, income, ownership-period or other product requirements to establish?

Ask the broker to help map the realistic process and its dependencies.

Some work can happen alongside the refurbishment. Other steps may have to wait for completion or specific evidence.

The point is to find that out while you still have time.

At Mozarts, the project ran beyond the original programme and required replacement development finance. The additional fees and interest were expected to cost roughly £50,000 or more.

Replacing finance can be a solution. It has to be available, affordable and achievable in time.

“Someone will refinance it” is not the same as an exit you have investigated.

### A twelve-month bridge can be shorter than it looks

On a small house that I intend to refurbish and sell, I prefer to allow fifteen months on the bridge rather than automatically taking twelve.

That is not because the building work should take fifteen months. It is because the loan has to cover the journey to sale completion.

A well-organised team might turn around a straightforward three-bedroom refurbishment in four to six weeks. But on a smaller project you may be coordinating individual trades, with gaps between visits and people fitting your work around other jobs.

What if somebody does not turn up? What if you need to replace a trade? What if the work takes six months?

Then you put it on the market. It takes three months and a couple of price reductions to agree a sale. The buyer's purchase takes another four months to complete.

| Stage | Illustrative time |
|---|---:|
| Refurbishment | 6 months |
| Marketing to an agreed sale | 3 months |
| Agreed sale to completion | 4 months |
| Total | 13 months |

You are a month beyond a twelve-month term.

The buyer may still intend to buy. The house may be finished. Neither repays the lender on the date the loan falls due.

If you cannot repay or agree an extension, you can face a funding shortfall and consequences under the loan terms. Rebridging may be an option, but it is another application, another cost and another timetable.

The existing loan may have an exit fee, while the replacement facility has an arrangement fee and further costs. A couple of thousand pounds at each end can take a noticeable bite out of a small flip's profit. Check which charges were already in your budget and which are genuinely additional.

You may also be borrowing on different terms. Do not assume the next lender will offer what the first one did.

That is why I like to add three months when arranging a bridge for this sort of project. I am building some room into the timetable before I need it.

Compare the actual terms, including extension arrangements, repayment charges and how interest is calculated. A fifteen-month term is a buffer, not a reason to stop managing the programme.

**Choose the term around when the lender gets repaid, not when the builder expects to finish.**

### Vivian Street: eight months just to buy it

One of our early purchases was number 8 Vivian Street in Swansea.

It took eight months to purchase.

The delay was on the seller's legal side. Whatever progress we thought we were making, we were still waiting to complete.

In that time, we could potentially have bought another house, refurbished it and refinanced it.

That experience is one reason I like a traditional auction purchase with a clear contractual completion deadline. On some deals that can be around a month, but you must read the actual auction conditions: the deadline and commitment depend on the contract and auction method.

The attraction is the defined process. The checks and funding still need to be ready for it.

Now imagine being on the other side of a slow transaction. You own the property. The work is done. Your buyer is taking longer than expected.

Meanwhile, your short-term finance keeps costing money.

Using the sort of monthly rates you may encounter, the interest on a £100,000 balance would be:

| Illustrative monthly rate | Interest for one month |
|---|---:|
| 1.0% | £1,000 |
| 1.1% | £1,100 |
| 1.2% | £1,200 |

Those are simple illustrations on an unchanged balance, before fees or any compounding. They are not product quotes.

Four additional months at 1.2% would be £4,800 on that balance before other costs. A larger loan makes the same delay more expensive.

If the delay also carries you past the finance term, the problem can become more than another month's interest.

So keep asking what is needed to complete the sale, who is dealing with it and whether the expected date still fits the funding.

**A buyer saying yes does not stop your interest bill. Completion does.**

### “We can always rent it out”

If a planned sale disappoints, renting may be a useful alternative.

But it is a separate business plan.

Can the property be lawfully let in the intended way? Does the funding permit it? What setup, compliance, management and insurance arrangements are needed?

Most importantly, does the rent cover the costs and leave an outcome you can accept?

Imagine a house built for resale with a relatively high total cost. The local rent may not support enough borrowing to repay the development loan and release the cash you need.

You might be able to hold it—but only by leaving in more money.

That is not necessarily a disaster. It does mean your backup depends on cash.

Run the rental appraisal before you describe it as the safety net.

### “We can always sell it”

The reverse needs testing too.

If you intend to refinance and hold, what happens if you need to sell?

Is there a realistic buyer market for the finished property? Would you be selling to an owner-occupier, an investor or a more specialist buyer? What evidence supports the likely price and selling period?

Would the sale clear the debt and costs? Would it return enough of your cash?

A property can be a good operating asset without being easy to sell quickly at the value you want.

You do not need to predict the exact buyer. You need to understand the market you may have to use.

### Give the backup a trigger

A backup is more useful when you know when you will act on it.

For example:

“If the refinance options investigated with the broker do not support the required net advance, I will reassess the sale route before committing to further works.”

Or:

“If the property has not attracted credible interest by the review date, we will reassess the marketing and price against the remaining finance term.”

Choose triggers that fit the deal. Work with your team on the actual dates.

Without a trigger, it is easy to spend another month hoping the first plan improves. That month can narrow the alternatives.

Keep the main route, backup and decision points together.

| Exit route | What you need to establish |
|---|---|
| Sell | Supported price, likely costs, debt repayment and a realistic selling process |
| Refinance | Product suitability, valuation basis, net advance and cash left invested |
| Hold | Appropriate funding, sustainable income, running costs and cash reserves |
| Replace short-term finance | Availability, net funds, total extra cost, term and a credible onward exit |

A second route is not automatically better because it is different. It has to survive its own appraisal.

### Make promises that match the uncertainty

If an investor's money is part of the deal, the exit timetable affects somebody else as well as you.

Be clear about the agreed arrangement, repayment obligations and what the project depends on.

Do not turn an expected refinance date into a certainty in conversation.

Keep investors informed as the project changes, and deal with agreements properly through the appropriate advice.

Surprising someone with a delay shortly before they expect their money back makes an already difficult situation harder.

The same discipline applies to your own commitments. Do not spend an expected capital release on paper three times before the lender has even valued the property.

### Your exit test

Before buying, write down:

1. **My main route:** sell, refinance or hold.
2. **The expected net result:** cash returned, debt remaining and money left invested.
3. **The conditions:** what must happen for that route to work.
4. **The timetable:** the steps and the date the current finance needs repaying.
5. **The weaker outcome:** what happens if the price, valuation or timing disappoints.
6. **My backup:** its costs, funding needs and the point at which I will act.

If the backup requires money you do not have, it is not yet a workable backup. If both routes depend on the same optimistic value, you may have one assumption wearing two different names.

You have now worked through the property, the market, the costs, the blockers, the delivery and the exit.

There is one last decision.

What do you do with all of it when the agent calls and asks whether you are making an offer?

**You need a price you can defend—and the discipline to stop at it.**
